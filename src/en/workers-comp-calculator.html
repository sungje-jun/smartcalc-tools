How Workers Compensation Works

Workers comp is a no-fault system — you don't need to prove your employer was negligent. In exchange, you generally can't sue your employer for the injury.

Benefit calculation: Most states pay 2/3 of your average weekly wage (AWW) for temporary total disability, subject to state minimums and maximums. Permanent partial disability uses your disability rating to determine the settlement.

Lump sum vs. structured: You can often negotiate a lump-sum settlement instead of weekly payments. Lump sums are typically discounted 10-20% but give you immediate access to funds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer for workers comp?

For simple claims with accepted injuries, you may not need one. But if your claim is denied, your employer disputes the injury, or you have a permanent disability, an attorney can significantly increase your settlement (typically 15-20% contingency fee).

Can I be fired for filing workers comp?

It's illegal to retaliate against an employee for filing a workers comp claim. However, your employer can terminate you for legitimate business reasons. Document everything and consult an attorney if you suspect retaliation.

What if my claim is denied?

You can appeal within 30-90 days (varies by state). Common denial reasons include late reporting, pre-existing conditions, or disputed work-relatedness. An attorney can help navigate the appeals process.
